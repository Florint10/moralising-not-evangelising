Based on the aforementioned theories by \textcite{repp_whats_2012}, \textcite{king_virtue_2017}, and \textcite{calleja_in_game_2011}, I created my own theoretical framework which gives two compatible explanations for why players might criticise overt instruction in digital games, whether they are Christian or not:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*]
	\item \textbf{The game is overly instructive such that players suspect that the knowledge or values presented by the designer are subject to intellectual blindspots.} \label{enum:intellectual_blindspots}
	\item \textbf{The game integrates informative elements in ways that hinder player engagement.} \label{enum:unengaging_edification}
\end{enumerate}

Based on the evidence I provided in Section~\ref{sec:warrant_reduction_assumption}, I reasoned that the Warrant Reduction Hypothesis could also be applied to digital games, leading me to write account~\ref{enum:intellectual_blindspots}. Meanwhile, account~\ref{enum:unengaging_edification} is a combination of the observations which King and I made for digital games. In other words, heavy-handed instruction, and the ways in which digital games deliver instruction more broadly, can both restrict the engagement one can derive from a game. I analyse the latter possibility through the lens of the Player Involvement Model (see Section~\ref{sec:player_involvement_model}).

One important detail that I would like to mention is that the reasons listed above can contribute to a negative experience of instructional elements \textit{simultaneously}. As I will demonstrate in Section~\ref{sec:alum_key_observations}, players have panned the scripted narrative in \textit{Alum} \parencite{orsie_alum_2006} not only because they suspected that Orsie might be prone to zealotry and prejudice; they also find fault the game's overtness in itself, with one player calling the scripted narrative ``insultingly obvious in its Christian intent'' \parencite[para. 1]{speedkillsuk_review_2017}.

Using the above framework, I performed a game analysis of two Christian games to understand how their formal elements contribute to player’s opinions. While I could have included further examples to help substantiate my claims, I would have run the risk of having to analyse them in a very superficial manner in doing so. Therefore, to keep the analysis insightful, I opted for a smaller sample of games.

For my first case study, I chose \textit{Alum} \parencite{orsie_alum_2006}, primarily due to the substantial of reviews decrying the game for the unsubtle delivery of its themes. I explore these reviews more thoroughly in Section~\ref{sec:alum_key_observations}, but, for reference, I have already discussed the review by \textcite{arkane_review_2015} in Section~\ref{sec:warrant_reduction_assumption}. There are also further examples such as the reviews by \textcite{adam_review_2015, baxted_review_2015, virotti_review_2017, stranger_review_2015}. Furthermore, I have already shown in Section~\ref{sec:my_definition} that the game complies with my own definition of Christian games.

I chose \textit{Captain Bible in Dome of Darkness} \parencite{engelbrite_captain_1994}, or \textit{Captain Bible}, as I will refer to it from now on, as my second case study. Like \textit{Alum}, it also fits my working definition of Christian games, given its clear Biblical theming and Christian moral lessons, which it delivers through interactive theological arguments against robotic enemies and deceived citizen \acp{NPC}. Furthermore, \textit{Captain Bible} is one of the few Christian games I am aware of which integrates moral instruction with gameplay in a comprehensive manner. 

To my knowledge, many Christian edutainment games are either ``digitised Sunday School exercises'' \parencite[60]{gonzalez_born-again_2014} such as virtual quizzes, crossword puzzles, jigsaw puzzles, word jumbles, colouring books and hangman (p.~119) or rehash existing genre conventions with the addition of Christian theming or, in the case of The Bible Game, multiple-choice questions relating to Christianity \parencite[288--289]{bogost_persuasive_2007}. Other games may also include Bible quotes (e.g., \textit{Bible Adventures} \parencite{wisdom_tree_bible_1991}, \textit{Catechumen} \parencite{bagley_catechumen_2000}, \textit{Left Behind: Eternal Forces} \parencite{inspired_media_entertainment_left_2006}).

Players of \textit{Captain Bible} also seem to view the game as unique in this way. While discussing the game, \textcite[Shucks, Why Do You Want Me To Play This? section, para.~5]{annk526_captain_2024} observes that ``there are not a lot of Christian Video Games [sic], and most of the ones people know are reskins or rehashes of other ideas with a `choose the right answer' style multiple choice that feels tacked on''. Furthermore, \textcite[para. 1]{censorious_idler_captain_2018} praises the game as ``a successful synthesis of the ludic (in Huizinga’s sense) and didactic principles necessitated by the video game form and its Evangelical heritage'', and mentions that few other Christian games do the same (In Retrospect section). Therefore, if the educational game design in \textit{Captain Bible} is somehow limiting player engagement, it should be rather obvious; more so than the example I gave using \textit{Bible Adventures} in Sections~\ref{sec:aesthetic_agency} and~\ref{sec:player_involvement_model}.

As I have already noted in Section~\ref{sec:player_involvement_model}, player involvement is influenced not only by game design by also by player subjectivity. Therefore, to prevent my analysis from becoming too subjective, I will supplement my analysis with player reviews taken from blogs and online platforms such as Steam and YouTube. Of these reviews, I will only be considering those written or spoken in English. I do this not only because of my familiarity with the language, but also to keep my research accessible.
